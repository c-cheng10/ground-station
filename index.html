            How it works

            The data

            All data comes through NASA’s Giovanni tool, and each measurement uses NASA’s most consistent record for it. Air temperature and humidity come from MERRA-2 (available since 1980), a weather model that incorporates observations. Precipitation comes from the GPM satellites (available since 1998). Soil moisture, evaporation, wind, snow and surface temperature come from the GLDAS 2.0 land model (1948–2014).

            Land samples are spaced about 3° apart and ocean samples about 5° apart. MERRA-2 provides total column ozone and tropopause temperature (since 1980). Daytime AIRS observations provide total column water vapour, cloud fraction, outgoing longwave radiation, and sea surface skin temperature (since September 2002).

            Use Magic select to sketch a ring around a place. The app selects the sampled points inside it. Each point counts equally, and the area chart uses months when every selected point has a value.

            Selection names describe the sampled grid points. Nearby cities and country outlines come from Natural Earth. Biome labels use sample centres within RESOLVE Ecoregions 2017 (CC BY 4.0).

            The trend test

            A straight line through the yearly averages gives the change per decade. Its range and p-value allow for one year’s weather leaning on the next. The Mann-Kendall test checks the answer a second way, with the Hamed–Rao correction when one year clearly leans on the next, and both are adjusted for testing every measurement available at that place at once (the Holm method).

            The heatmap estimates trends between nearby sampled points on land and groups them into nine bands of equal width. Each point on the globe uses the same trend test as the results panel, with a correction for checking about 1,700 places at once (the Benjamini–Hochberg method). Because the panel corrects for the measurements at one place instead, the two can occasionally differ; the panel says so when they do.

            Countries lift above the globe when you select an area. Taller peaks show larger changes, and colour shows whether the measurement rose or fell. The charts use the grid points in the selection.

            The forecast

            A world model learned how air temperature, humidity and rain push each other from month to month, and how El Niño in the Pacific nudges them. It reads the last 12 months plus the last 5 years’ average, predicts next month, then feeds that prediction back in to reach 12 months.

            It was tested on 2020–2025 at 350 locations held out from training. The 2001–2020 anomaly baseline includes the first test year.

            Honest limits

            A trend can be real and still not reach significance yet; a longer record often settles it. Records have different lengths, so compare trends from the same years. With only a few decades of years, p-values are approximate, especially for precipitation (27 years).

            Trends are straight lines, so sudden jumps or curves aren’t modelled. Most measurements come from NASA models built from observations (MERRA-2, GLDAS); only precipitation is measured directly from space (GPM). Two rules of thumb are not formal tests: datasets “agree” when their trends are within 0.15 °C per decade, and the forecast’s shaded band assumes errors spread like those seen in testing.

            The newer GLDAS 2.1 record runs to 2025, but its inputs changed over time and it shows false warming in places like the Sahel and India. That’s why it only appears in the “Do the datasets agree?” check. The forecast knows nothing about greenhouse gases, so it can’t say where the climate is headed decades from now.
