    What do these numbers mean?
    Change per decade
    How much the value rises or falls every 10 years, read from the straight trend line.
    Likely range (95%)
    The real rate of change probably sits somewhere in this range. If the range crosses zero, the data can’t tell rising from falling.
    p-value
    The chance of seeing a trend this strong if nothing were really changing. Scientists usually call a trend significant when it is 0.05 (1 in 20) or smaller.
    Mann-Kendall and Theil-Sen
    A second test that only asks whether later years tend to be higher or lower than earlier ones, so one extreme year can’t fool it. When both tests agree, you can trust the result more.
    Why the p-values are adjusted
    Testing 8 measurements at once raises the odds that one looks significant by luck. The tool raises the bar to make up for that (the Holm method).

    How the measurements move together

    Each number runs from −1 to +1. Near +1, the two rise and fall together. Near −1, one rises when the other falls. Near 0, there’s no clear link. The tool removes the seasons and the long-term trend first, so you only see month-to-month surprises.

    A link is not proof that one causes the other.

    Where the data comes from

    Every number comes from NASA POWER, which serves MERRA-2: NASA’s reanalysis, a weather model that is continuously corrected with satellite and ground observations. The tool asks for the 9 grid cells around your chosen point (each about 50 km across) and averages them, giving more weight to cells that cover more area.

    Reanalysis rainfall can shift when the satellites feeding the model change, so treat rainfall trends with extra care.

    How the math works

    The tool fits a straight line through the yearly averages from 1981 to 2025. Its 95% range and p-value allow for the way one year’s weather leans on the next (Newey–West errors). The Mann-Kendall test and Theil-Sen slope give a second opinion built on ranks and medians. Because 8 measurements are tested at once, both sets of p-values are adjusted with the Holm method, and a trend is called rising or falling only when its adjusted p-value is 0.05 or below.

    Monthly anomalies show how far each month was from the 1991–2020 average for the same calendar month, which removes the seasons.
