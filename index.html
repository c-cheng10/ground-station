        What do these numbers mean?
        Change per decade
        How much the value rises or falls every 10 years, read from the straight trend line.
        Likely range (95%)
        The real rate of change probably sits in this range. If it crosses zero, the data can’t tell rising from falling.
        p-value
        The chance of seeing a trend this strong if nothing were really changing. Scientists usually call 0.05 (1 in 20) or smaller significant.
        Mann-Kendall and Theil-Sen
        A second test that only asks whether later years tend to be higher or lower than earlier ones, so one extreme year can’t fool it.
        Why p-values are adjusted
        Testing 9 measurements at once raises the odds that one looks significant by luck, so the tool raises the bar (the Holm method).

        How the measurements move together

        Each number runs from −1 to +1. Near +1, the two rise and fall together. Near −1, one rises when the other falls. Near 0, there’s no clear link. Seasons and the long-term trend are removed first, and each pair is compared over the years both records cover. A link is not proof that one causes the other.

            How it works

            The data

            All data comes through NASA’s Giovanni tool, and each measurement uses NASA’s most consistent record for it. Air temperature and humidity come from MERRA-2 (available since 1980), a weather model that incorporates observations. Precipitation comes from the GPM satellites (available since 1998). Soil moisture, evaporation, wind, snow and surface temperature come from the GLDAS 2.0 land model (1948–2014).

            The database holds 1,656 land points. The map shows a trend for each point and measurement with enough data.

            Draw circle selects every grid point whose centre falls inside the outline on this map. Each point counts equally. The area chart uses months when every selected point has a value.

            The trend test

            A straight line through the yearly averages gives the change per decade. Its range and p-value allow for one year’s weather leaning on the next. The Mann-Kendall test checks the answer a second way, and both are adjusted for testing 9 measurements at once (the Holm method).

            On the map, each square uses the Mann-Kendall test, corrected for checking about 1,700 places at once (the Benjamini–Hochberg method). Faded squares didn’t pass.

            The forecast

            A world model learned how air temperature, humidity and rain push each other from month to month, and how El Niño in the Pacific nudges them. It reads the last 12 months plus the last 5 years’ average, predicts next month, then feeds that prediction back in to reach 12 months.

            It was tested on 2020–2025 at 350 locations held out from training. The 2001–2020 anomaly baseline includes the first test year.

            Honest limits

            A trend can be real and still not reach significance yet; a longer record often settles it. Records have different lengths, so compare trends from the same years.

            The newer GLDAS 2.1 record runs to 2025, but its inputs changed over time and it shows false warming in places like the Sahel and India. That’s why it only appears in the “Do the datasets agree?” check. The forecast knows nothing about greenhouse gases, so it can’t say where the climate is headed decades from now.
