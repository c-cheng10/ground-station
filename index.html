    What happens next?

    What do these numbers mean?
    Change per decade
    How much the value rises or falls every 10 years, read from the straight trend line.
    Likely range (95%)
    The real rate of change probably sits in this range. If it crosses zero, the data can’t tell rising from falling.
    p-value
    The chance of seeing a trend this strong if nothing were really changing. Scientists usually call 0.05 (1 in 20) or smaller significant.
    Mann-Kendall and Theil-Sen
    A second test that only asks whether later years tend to be higher or lower than earlier ones, so one extreme year can’t fool it.
    Why p-values are adjusted
    Testing 8 measurements at once raises the odds that one looks significant by luck, so the tool raises the bar (the Holm method).

    How the measurements move together

    Each number runs from −1 to +1. Near +1, the two rise and fall together. Near −1, one rises when the other falls. Near 0, there’s no clear link. Seasons and the long-term trend are removed first, so you only see month-to-month surprises. A link is not proof that one causes the other.

    How it works

    The same steps a climate scientist takes, done for you in a few seconds.

    The data

    Every number comes from NASA POWER, which serves MERRA-2: a weather model continuously corrected with satellite and ground observations, from 1981 to today.

    The tool averages the 9 grid cells around your point, each about 50 km across. Results are saved in your browser, and Refresh adds newly finished months to the team’s shared Supabase dataset for the 6 study areas.

    The trend test

    A straight line through the yearly averages gives the change per decade. Its range and p-value allow for one year’s weather leaning on the next.

    The Mann-Kendall test checks the answer a second way. With 8 measurements tested at once, both are adjusted with the Holm method.

    The forecast

    A world model learned how the 8 measurements push each other from month to month: rain wets the soil, wet soil cools the surface, and so on. It reads the last 12 months plus the last 5 years’ average, predicts next month, then feeds that prediction back in to reach 12 months.

    It trained on 6 regions from 1986 to 2019 and was tested on 2020–2025, which it never saw. A small neural network was tried too, but the simpler linear model forecast better, so the site uses that. Forecasts fade toward the recent average after a few months, because the weather’s memory runs out.

    Honest limits

    A trend can be real and still not reach significance yet; a longer record often settles it. Rainfall from weather models can shift when the satellites feeding them change.

    The forecast knows nothing about greenhouse gases, so it can’t say where the climate is headed decades from now. For that, use the trend line and its range.
